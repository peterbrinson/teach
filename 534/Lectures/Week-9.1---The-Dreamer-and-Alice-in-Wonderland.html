What Is Theme?

Theme is the idea a work is about beneath its events — a claim, question, or insight about life that the work keeps returning to without stating outright.

It helps to separate it from its neighbors:

  • Subject — what the work depicts: a boy, his father, a favela, a monster.
  • Theme — what the work says or asks about that: in Papo & Yo, how a child learns to live with, and finally let go of, a parent’s addiction.
  • Metaphor — the vehicle. One thing carries the qualities of another: the Monster is the father; the frogs are the drink.
  • Subtext — the layer where theme lives: meaning that sits below the surface and has to be read rather than told.

So the chain runs: decipher a metaphor → it exposes the subtext → where the theme is found.

Two tests for whether you’ve named a theme:

  1. It survives a change of subject. “A child cannot fix their parent” could be told with a different boy, a different monster, or no game at all.
  2. It’s an idea, not a topic. “Addiction” is a topic. What the work says or asks about addiction is the theme.

In games, The Dreamer adds a twist: theme isn’t only read, it’s performed. When solving the puzzle requires acting out the metaphor — feeding the Monster, then being hurt by him — the player arrives at the theme through their own decisions, while the comprehension becomes their own.















We can read subtext after we decipher a metaphor. A metaphor is a comparison between two things that are otherwise unrelated. With metaphor, the qualities of one thing are figuratively carried over to another.

The Benefits of Metaphors











Metaphors are a fundamental element of creative expression in literature, poetry, art, and other forms of artistic communication. They allow artists to convey complex emotions and ideas in novel and engaging ways.











Inside

Metaphors make information more memorable because they create mental associations between concepts. People are more likely to remember a metaphorical statement than a straightforward factual one.

Remember L.O.P.

  1. Legibility. The player enters a room with a locked or blocked exit. Some of the room’s affordances† are not imminently legible. The puzzle solver tests what they can do. What do objects and items do? What parts of the space can the player access? That is, how is the space a puzzle? Indeed, sense-making is possible.
  2. Opportunity. They form a solution in their mind. Is this correct?
  3. Performance. They execute. Can the player do it?

† Affordance is an action a person perceives as possible. Think of a doorknob.

Surrealism was a European art movement from about 100 years ago. It featured illogic to draw attention to the unconscious mind. Interpreting and configuring the work into a logical meaning is the role of the viewer.

Perhaps more than any other genre, it features surprise. (Hey that sounds familiar…!?)
