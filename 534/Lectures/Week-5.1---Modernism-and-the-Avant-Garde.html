The Mind as a Puzzle

The Mind

The Conscious and Unconscious

(Let’s interpret: what is the main character thinking?)

Freud’s is an essential figure in the 20th Century. We can thank him and Carl Yung for psychoanalysis, and perhaps by extension, therapy. Most of his specific ideas are passe, for example his interpretations themselves, such as penis envy and so on.   But something rings true with his distinction and relationship between the conscious and unconscious mind. The mind, a person’s thoughts, memories and personality, are a puzzle to be solved, to be interpreted. Let us ponder if the unconscious explains most of our behavior. And next: it takes work to get to know ourselves. We have repressed thoughts, from prior experiences. They cause unexpected behavior, and dreams, no doubt.   This is familiar. Movies seem to work the same way. This is rich stuff for the filmmaker. As viewers, it seems to be our role to interpret the motivation of the characters, the possibilities for outcomes, and indeed what the film means.











[In traditional stories], the dramatic question [gets answered at the end]. We’ll leave the story with that lovely emotional sense that something, perhaps just beyond the level of conscious comprehension, has been completed.

Modernist stories are different.

Storr, Will. The Science of Storytelling: Why Stories Make Us Human and How to Tell Them Better (pp. 128-129). Abrams Press. Kindle Edition.

Instructor notes

Read aloud: Modernist stories are different. Whilst they’re built from the same dance between surface drama and subconscious change, their causes and effects are often left ambiguous. Character change occurs, but it’s less clear how these changes are being triggered by the drama and what message we’re supposed to glean from them. More space is left for the reader to insert their own interpretations into the text.

Franz Kafka’s short story ‘The Passenger’ shows an enigmatic movement of cause and effect between consciousness and subconscious. It tells of a man on a tram feeling uncertain about himself and his place in the world. He becomes lost, for a moment, in the abstract physical details of a woman waiting to disembark – the position of her hands, the shape of her nose, the shadow her ear makes against her skull. These conscious observations trigger something deep in his subconscious. He asks, ‘How is it that she is not astonished at herself, that she keeps her mouth closed, and expresses nothing of any wonderment?’ The reader is invited to ponder how one level connects to the other and thereby bring them into harmony.
