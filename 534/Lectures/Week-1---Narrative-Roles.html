Week 1 - Narrative Roles

Video games use image, sound, and text to communicate. This session sets up the cinematic vocabulary we will use all semester, then introduces four narrative roles — The Investigator, The Traveler, The Entrant, and The Dreamer — that organize your first three projects.

Narrative Roles

Your first three assignments are framed by a sequence of narrative roles.

Player’s experiences are shaped by the role they are given – what the player can and does do.

This class will study four such roles with pragmatism:

three provide constraints for assignments during our first 10 weeks, while The Entrant clarifies the transition from discovery to control.











The Protagonist

Horizon Zero Dawn (2017).

First, What is a main character?

The most common cinematic storytelling framework centers around the protagonist, the pursuit of most professional storytellers from all mediums.











Star Wars: Return of the Jedi (1983).

The Protagonist

Get Out (2017).

The Protagonist faces danger or crisis often embodied in other characters. As the player struggles against antagonists (and sometimes themselves), their relationship to the concept of control provides the conduit for our empathy, and by extension, how we engage the story’s themes.

Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon (2000).

Ghost in the Shell (1995).











Genshin Impact (2020).

God of War (2018).

We feel empathy towards protagonists. This dynamic operates as a cycle found in popular literature and Hollywood films: we get to know our character’s internal reality by seeing the choices they make under stress.

Final Fantasy VII Remake (2020).

The Last of Us Part II (2020).











Once we understand what they want, we anticipate what they might do, and we also witness good and bad things happening to them.

Therefore, the story is composed of a range of strong and mild surprises.

The cycle repeats with different or progressed dilemmas.

Bones and All (2022).











Sometimes the character must discover their internal reality in order to control their external one.

The Matrix (1999).











Sound of Metal (2019).

Other times, after they fail to control their external world, they discover insight: perhaps they learn that they were not struggling against others, but against themselves.











Baba Is You (2019).

No doubt, Game Design includes providing players the experiences of discovery and control











But narrative games complicate the protagonist paradigm.

In such video games, there are two main characters: the protagonist and the player.

This is why this course isolates four roles for our early assignments.

The assignments frame storytelling through what the player can and does do.











The Investigator

Tacoma (2017).

Assignment 1: The Investigator











The Investigator

Firewatch (2016).

The Investigator role embraces much of what we know from the detective and mystery genres of literature and cinema.

The character searches for clues and engages other characters to discover the story.

The game’s plot is the player’s attempt at gathering information, and the story is the resulting sequence of events we mentally, and retrospectively, stitch together in our minds.

L.A. Noire (2011).

Chinatown (1974).











The Investigator role provides an excellent framework for environmental storytelling.











Dark Souls III (2016), Firelink Shrine — facing the five thrones of the Lords of Cinder.

Dark Souls III is not focused on The Investigator, but it repeatedly creates Investigator experiences. The player reads item descriptions and messages, inspects objects, listens to NPCs, and studies architecture and remains to reconstruct what happened.

The environmental designer embeds clues, events, and characters in the world. They are part level designer, architect, and teller of tales.











*Installation at The Broad, Los Angeles

The Investigator

We find such storytelling in a myriad of forms, including themed entertainment (such as Disneyland), escape rooms, alternate reality games, and museums.











The Investigator

Global Conflicts: Palestine.

Because The Investigator primarily discovers (rather than controls), this role provides straightforward mechanics for real-world topics.











Her Story (2015) — a story discovered in the past tense.

Games featuring the other three roles tell their stories in the present tense.

But The Investigator discovers the story that happened in the past.











Plot and story

Detective fiction gives us the cleanest version of this distinction, and it is worth naming precisely.

  • The plot is the order in which the player encounters the investigation.
  • The story is the underlying sequence of events, assembled retrospectively.

Chinatown and The Maltese Falcon both work this way. The detective changes very little in the world. The clues, the motives, and the relationships are all in place before the film begins — what changes over the course of it is comprehension.

Designing for The Investigator means designing the plot: deciding what the player finds, and in what order, so that the story assembles itself in their head.

Maze and Labyrinth

We use these two words in a specific way:

  • A maze is what the word normally evokes — a puzzle of pathways, designed to be solved.
  • A labyrinth is a maze plus meaning. It is architecture built to carry a story or a metaphor.

The Traveler moves through a labyrinth, not a maze. The difference is whether the space is something to solve or something to undergo.

Umberto Eco separates mazes by how they branch:

  • Unicursal — one winding path, no choices. You cannot get lost, because there is only one route.
  • Multicursal — branches, dead ends, and decisions at every intersection.
  • Network — every point can connect to many others. No fixed start, no fixed end.

The Entrant

Situated Player Role 3: The Entrant

The Entrant has arrived at a boundary.

Unlike The Traveler, who moves through space as an interpretive act, The Entrant confronts a blocked passage. Progress is possible — but only by satisfying conditions.

The player asks: How does this world work?











Uncharted.

The Entrant reads what is present, tests what the system accepts or rejects, and gradually learns the rules governing passage.

The threshold might be a locked door, a puzzle room, a boss, a border, or a bureaucratic procedure.











Portal (2007) — the chamber itself is a spatial threshold.

In Portal, passage is not blocked by a conventional locked door. The player must reorganize space by creating a non-Euclidean pathway through the chamber.

The player does not merely find the path, as The Traveler might. They must understand and manipulate the room’s system to make passage possible.











Papers, Please (2013) — a bureaucratic threshold.

Not every threshold is architectural. In Papers, Please, the player becomes the threshold: inspecting documents, applying changing rules, and deciding who may cross the border.

The act of accepting or rejecting passage becomes the story.











The Shift to Control

The Entrant marks the framework’s major shift from discovery toward control.

Agency is no longer expressed merely through movement or interpretation, but by demonstrating alignment with the system’s demands.











The Entrant’s loop: L.O.P.

The puzzle-platformer Inside (2016) consistently manifests a repeatable three-step loop: Legibility, Opportunity, and Performance.

Inside (2016) — Legibility.

Legibility: See what matters. The player recognizes the barrier and identifies the interactive components in the room.











Inside (2016) — Opportunity.

Opportunity: Imagine the solution. By experimenting with individual mechanics, the player imagines how the components might be configured or sequenced.











Inside (2016) — Performance.

Performance: Test the idea. The player attempts to carry out the imagined solution. The system accepts or rejects the performance.

The Investigator learns what happened. The Traveler reaches the boundary. The Entrant tests whether understanding and action are sufficient to cross it.

The threshold does more than halt progress: it defines the player’s relationship to the world and its rules.

How do the mechanics let the player imagine and perform a solution?

The Dreamer

John Tenniel’s illustration for Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland (1865).

Assignment 3: The Dreamer











Humanity (2023).

Inception (2010).

The Dreamer

Inside (2016).

The Dreamer strives to control their environment, but unlike The Entrant — who confronts a material reality — The Dreamer confronts a symbolic one.

The world expresses an internal state, and the player must interpret that expression in order to act.

The player asks: What does this world express?

Baba Is You (2019).

Braid (2008).











The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920).

With this role, the world is rich and vibrant.

Like in expressionistic cinema from early 20th century, the world holds the intrigue.











The Witness (2016).

At the level of mechanics, a Dreamer game may resemble an Entrant game: both can use puzzles.

The distinction is not whether the player solves a puzzle, but whether the puzzle also functions as metaphor.











Alice in Wonderland (Disney, 1951).

Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland provides an excellent entry point for the shift between The Entrant and The Dreamer.

The Entrant approach: Alice tries a key in a locked door. The problem follows grounded logic, but she is too large to pass through.

The Dreamer approach: she must drink from a bottle and eat cake, changing her body and accepting the dream logic of the world.

The Entrant’s threshold demands mastery of how the world works. The Dreamer’s threshold demands recognition of what the world expresses.











Everything Everywhere All at Once (2022).

_We take on the same task as our character –

interpreting images in order to take the next steps.

_Such images are both symbolic and a piece of the puzzle.











Papo & Yo (2012).

The task of interpreting what the thing can do…

How is that connected to interpreting what it means?











Humanity (2023).

But it is not only the puzzle pieces that are symbolic, but (like with our own dreams), the overall experience is worth interpreting.











Paprika (2006) — a world that is explicitly an externalized dream.

In some cases, the worlds are not merely dream-like but are explicitly externalized dreams.











Inside (2016).

We might not have access to The Dreamer’s thoughts in the way we expect from a traditional protagonist.

In this case, the player character is like a blank canvas for what we are thinking, therefore the avatar’s visual rendering might lack detail.











The Alice Shift

Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland shows the exact moment The Entrant becomes The Dreamer. Alice is stuck in a hallway, behind a locked door only ten inches high.

  1. The Entrant approach — rationality. She tries the key in the locks. It fits the small door, but she cannot fit through it. She reasons about keys and doors as material things.
  2. The Dreamer approach — surrealism. She goes back to the table and finds a bottle labeled “DRINK ME.” To get through, she has to accept the logic the world is offering and change her own body to match it.

The Entrant’s threshold asks the player to master how the world works. The Dreamer’s threshold asks them to recognize what the world is expressing.

The L.O.P. loop still runs here — but legibility becomes the hard part rather than the first step. In Papo & Yo, the puzzles are a metaphor for living with an alcoholic parent. The player still solves them; the meaning is in what solving them reveals. The mechanics are the language.
