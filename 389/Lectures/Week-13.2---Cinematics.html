Week 13.2 — Cinematics

Week 13.2 — Cinematics

Today’s agenda — Cinematics (techniques for creating cutscene cinematics in Unity), Portfolios (looking at portfolio pages from students and recent graduates), and the Salmond Reading discussion.

A workshop session: walk through Unity’s Timeline + Recorder for building cutscene cinematics, then look at example portfolio pages from recent students and graduates, then discuss Sylvester’s Engines of Experience chapter.











Interactive Lights and Cameras

Let’s open up Unity! Download and open the Timeline demo project.











Timeline — opening the window

Open the Timeline window and dock it somewhere convenient.

Window > Sequencing > Timeline

I like to dock the Timeline at the bottom of the editor window, along with the Project and Console windows. It’s useful to have a wide space to work with.











Timeline Asset

A timeline needs to be connected to both a GameObject on the Hierarchy and an asset in the project files.

Create an empty GameObject and call it Timeline. When you select it, the Timeline window will prompt you to create the Timeline asset. Save this asset somewhere appropriate in your project folder.











Lock Window View

Once you create your Timeline asset, you’ll want to lock your Timeline window to the Timeline GameObject. (Otherwise, it will try to show a new timeline whenever you select a different GameObject.)

Click the lock icon on the top right hand corner of the Timeline window.











Animation Track

An animation track can change the transform of a GameObject and interpolate between different transform values.

Drag any GameObject into the left side of the Timeline and select Animation Track. For example, you can create an animation track for a camera.

Click on the record button on the animation track to begin recording. Move the playhead to a time on the timeline and adjust the object’s transform to create a keyframe at that point. Move the playhead and create another keyframe, and the timeline will animate between them.











Activation Track

An activation track controls whether a GameObject is active or not.

Drag any GameObject into the left side of the Timeline and select Activation Track.

Right click on the timeline track and select Add Activation Clip. Adjust the starting and ending times of the clip on the timeline. During times when the clip is on the timeline, the GameObject will be active; whenever the clip is not on the timeline, the GameObject will be inactive.











Audio Track

An audio track can control what audio file plays from an Audio Source.

Drag an AudioSource into the left side of the Timeline and select Audio Track.

Right click on the timeline track and select Add From Audio Clip, or drag an audio asset directly into the timeline track. You can adjust the starting point of the track and see how the waveform will line up with other elements on your timeline. Remember that you can only play one audio file from an Audio Source at a time.











Unity Recorder

One more thing: Unity has a native recorder component that you can use to capture frames or video of your game. You have to download and install the Recorder package from the package manager, but it’s very easy to use.

Once you’ve installed the recorder, you can open the Recorder window from:

Window > General > Recorder > Recorder Window

When you start the recorder, it will automatically enter play mode and play your game. The recording will stop when you exit play mode. You can also set the recorder to automatically exit play mode if you end the recording from the Recorder window.

Add a new recorder. Make it a Movie Recorder. Check the settings. It will default to the same resolution as the Game window and save the video to a Recordings folder at the top level of your project.











Activity — Make a Cinematic

In small groups, use the Timeline and post processing effects to create a small cinematic cutscene. You should animate the camera and some lights.

You can use any assets you have access to, or grab some from https://www.kenney.nl/assets/category:3D.

Record your cinematic using the Unity Recorder and post it to the Discord.











Portfolios — approach as a design problem

A portfolio is interactive media! Approach your portfolio as a design problem, the same way that you would approach designing a game.

First step: What’s your experience goal? Think about what you want someone to understand about you when they look at your portfolio.

How can you present your work to create that understanding?

Flip the script

We don’t often talk about it in these terms, but flipping the script is a vital design skill. As a game designer, you have to think about what your game looks like to a brand new player. You’ve developed the skills to put yourself in that person’s shoes, even though you know a lot more about the game than they do.

Put yourself in the shoes of the person who’s looking at your portfolio. What do they want? What are they looking for? How much time are they going to spend looking at your website?

Use role-playing and playtesting to help get into the head of a reviewer!











Show your process

A potential employer is not just concerned with your product; in fact, they care even more about your process. Of course you want to put games that you’re proud of up on your portfolio, but it’s important that you explain what you contributed to the game and how you do your work.

Show your process. This will give readers who understand design confidence that you also have good practices. It also demystifies design for readers who don’t have a lot of experience with these processes. And it can help turn a lackluster product into a valuable selling point.
