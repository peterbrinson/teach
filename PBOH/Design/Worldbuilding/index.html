Bounded Worlds: The Playable and the Imagined

Bounded Worlds: The Playable and the Imagined

1. What Is The Bounded Worlds Framework?

Bounded Worlds is a game design framework that foregrounds the world as a bounded space — expressive through the world's edge.

Every game design project faces the same challenge: our initial ideas are too large to build. The Bounded Worlds framework provides a direct response. It encourages worlds that are expressive through their limits rather than their size.

The Bounded Worlds framework is the worldbuilding companion to the Situated Player Roles: where the player roles turn on what the player does, Bounded Worlds turns on how the world defines its edges.

Because the world has overt limits, the designer can focus on making it consistent, inhabited, and intelligible. Bounded Worlds frames (Brian Upton’s) situations as the essential components of worldbuilding: as the player chooses a path through them, exploration expresses their choices within constraint.

2. Which Boundary Defines My World?

Every Bounded World has one of three Boundaries — Finite, Closed, or Threshold.

There are a few ways a Bounded World can treat its edges. Some feel complete in themselves. Some present edges that can be approached but not passed. Others are structured around boundaries the player can cross.

For your Bounded Worlds project, your foundational decision: does the world imply or provide an outside?
If it doesn’t, the world is Finite. If it does, ask whether the player can reach it — an outside implied but withheld is Closed; an edge the player passes through is a Threshold.

Finite

The world presents itself as whole. The player engages entirely within what is present — there is no world outside of what’s playable.

Examples

  • Balance (1989) — the world is the platform and the five characters
  • Monument Valley (2014) — a set of micro-worlds, each small and finite
  • Mountain (2014) — you are a mountain
  • Inscryption (2021) — Act 1: the table, the cabin, the game
  • 12 Minutes (2021) — three rooms, one looping conversation; the world is the apartment. The world exists for 12 minutes
  • Thomas Was Alone (2012) — abstract rectangles in an empty field; the puzzle-space is the whole world
  • Journey (2012) — a single pilgrimage from desert to summit; nothing exists outside the arc
  • Tetris (1984) — the well and the falling shapes, a closed abstract system
  • Aesop’s Fables — each fable a tiny self-contained world, complete in a few lines (see The Boy Who Cried Wolf)

-A transition from Finite to Bounded: Yertle the Turtle (1958) — expanding the boundary

Closed

The player can encounter the edge of the world. The boundary is found during play — the system holds the player within its limits — but cannot be passed.

Examples

  • Escape rooms — sealed by design; the walls hold you for the duration of play
  • Terrariums, zoos, prisons — a world inside the world
  • Disneyland — the berm and landscaping hide the outside world from view; once inside, every sightline stays in-theme
  • The Exterminating Angel (Luis Buñuel, 1962) - bourgeois characters are stuck in their own world, a house
  • Dr Langeskov, The Tiger, and The Terribly Cursed Emerald: A Whirlwind Heist - backstage from the real (and inaccessible) game
  • Return of the Obra Dinn (2018) — the ship is the playable enclosure; ports, empire, weather, and voyage exist as evidence
  • Papers, Please (2013) — the checkpoint booth holds the player while Arstotzka’s politics press in through papers, guards, news, and family consequences
  • Tacoma (2017) — the station is explorable, but Earth, corporate power, and the crew’s lives remain outside the accessible corridors, readable only through traces
  • Gone Home (2013) — the storm-lit house is the world; the family’s wider life is implied through rooms, objects, and absences
  • BioShock (2007) — Rapture is sealed by the ocean; the surface exists as memory, ideology, and almost-impossible escape
  • Snowpiercer (Bong Joon-ho, 2013) — the train holds the last of humanity; outside is death, therefore the cars cannot be left
  • Flatland (Edwin Abbott, 1884) — two-dimensional beings who cannot reach the third dimension they are shown
  • The Platform (Galder Gaztelu-Urrutia, 2019) — a vertical prison-pit; the structure holds each prisoner to their level
  • La Maison en Petits Cubes (Kunio Katō, 2008) — a whole life stacked as rooms, read by diving down through them; it would be Finite but for the moment other structures appear in the distance — an outside that plainly exists, and that he never ventures to
  • P.T. (Kojima Productions, 2014) — an L-shaped corridor that loops on itself; you walk forward but never leave
  • Slaughterhouse-Five (Kurt Vonnegut, 1969) — Billy Pilgrim is kept in an alien zoo;

Threshold

The player can approach and pass from one state or space into another.

Examples

  • Alice in Wonderland (Lewis Carroll, 1865) — down the rabbit-hole into a world with its own logic
  • The Matrix (Wachowski Sisters, 1999) — crossing between the simulation and the real
  • Inception (Christopher Nolan, 2010) — descent through nested dream layers, each a world within a world
  • Paprika (Satoshi Kon, 2006) — the boundary between dream and waking dissolves
  • Portal (2007) — the portal itself is the crossing: step through one space, emerge from another
  • Psychonauts (2005) — you cross from the physical camp into the interior worlds of other minds
  • World of Tomorrow (Don Hertzfeldt, 2015) — a child pulled across time into a distant, stranger future
  • Spirited Away (Miyazaki, 2001) — Chihiro passes through the tunnel into the spirit world, before crossing back
  • Piranesi (Susanna Clarke, 2020) — the labyrinthine house connects to our world through hidden ways
  • Pan’s Labyrinth (Guillermo del Toro, 2006) — Ofelia moves between wartime Spain and the faun’s underground kingdom
  • Twin Peaks (1990–1991, 2017) — the town opens onto the Red Room, another order of reality
  • Severance (2022–) — the threshold splits work-self from home-self; crossing it changes who you are
  • Hades (2020) — each escape attempt crosses out of the Underworld toward the surface
  • The Chronicles of Narnia (C.S. Lewis, 1950–1956) — through the wardrobe into Narnia and back again

A Boundary describes the player’s experience of the edge, not a property of the world itself. Judge it from inside the player’s perspective — a locked door only reads as Closed if the player feels it as sealed — not from an outside description of “the thing” (the room, the ship, the pocket dimension). And a Boundary can change over the course of play: a game might hold Closed for its first hour, then open into a Threshold once a door finally gives way. Naming a Boundary describes the experience right now, not a fixed label for the whole game.

3. What Fundamentals Will Shape Your Design?

A handful of design concepts shape how the player meets, inhabits, and reads the world.

Meeting the World

How a player first enters a Bounded World is itself a designed moment: reading the boundary, sensing the place, an entry that can carry awe, dread, or curiosity.

Responsiveness

A world feels inhabited when it answers the player with memory, behavior, and consequence; it’s where the concept meets the practical work of building it.

Teaching The Rules

A world that runs on invented rules has to teach them, ideally through low-stakes play rather than a tutorial that pauses to explain.

Catalysts of Speculation

The deliberate gaps the player’s imagination fills

Awkward Exposition

Show don’t tell.

Go Deeper: Wolf’s Vocabulary
